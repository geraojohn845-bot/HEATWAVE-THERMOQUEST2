MODULE 01 | FUNDAMENTALS

Introduction to Thermodynamics

Discover how heat, work, temperature, and energy explain the behavior of physical systems.

College Level Estimated Time: 30–40 Minutes

Learning Objectives

At the end of this module, students should be able to:

  • Define thermodynamics and explain its importance in physics.
  • Differentiate heat, temperature, work, and internal energy.
  • Identify a thermodynamic system, surroundings, and boundary.
  • Classify open, closed, and isolated systems.
  • Explain practical applications of thermodynamics.

1.1 Understanding Thermodynamics

Thermodynamics is a branch of physics that studies the relationships among heat, temperature, work, and energy. It explains how energy is transferred and transformed in physical and chemical processes.

The principles of thermodynamics are important in understanding engines, refrigerators, power plants, industrial equipment, and many natural processes.

Key Idea

Energy can be transferred and transformed. Thermodynamics provides the scientific principles used to analyze these changes.

1.2 Fundamental Concepts

Heat

Energy transferred between systems because of a temperature difference.

Temperature

A physical property related to the average kinetic energy of particles.

Work

Energy transferred when a force acts through a displacement or through other generalized thermodynamic interactions.

Internal Energy

The microscopic kinetic and potential energy associated with the particles of a system.

1.3 Thermodynamic Systems

A thermodynamic system is a specified quantity of matter or a region in space selected for analysis. Everything outside the system is called the surroundings. The surface separating them is called the boundary.

System

The matter or region being studied.

Surroundings

Everything outside the selected system.

Boundary

The real or imaginary surface separating the system from its surroundings.

1.4 Types of Thermodynamic Systems

Open System

Exchanges both matter and energy with its surroundings.

Example: Boiling water in an uncovered pot.

Closed System

Exchanges energy but not matter with its surroundings.

Example: Gas inside a sealed piston.

Isolated System

Ideally exchanges neither matter nor energy with its surroundings.

Example: An ideal perfectly insulated sealed container.

1.5 Practical Applications

Thermodynamics is applied in several technologies and everyday activities.

  • Automobile engines convert thermal energy into mechanical work.
  • Refrigerators transfer heat from a colder space to warmer surroundings using work input.
  • Power plants convert energy into electrical power.
  • Cooking involves heat transfer and changes in the internal energy of food.
  • Air-conditioning systems regulate indoor temperature.

Module Summary

Thermodynamics studies heat, temperature, work, and energy. Understanding systems, surroundings, boundaries, and system classifications provides the foundation for studying the laws of thermodynamics.

MODULE 02 | THERMAL SCIENCE

Temperature and Heat

Understand the difference between temperature and heat, explore temperature scales, and calculate thermal energy transferred between substances.

College Level Estimated Time: 35–45 Minutes

Learning Objectives

At the end of this module, students should be able to:

  • Differentiate heat from temperature.
  • Explain the relationship between temperature and particle motion.
  • Convert temperatures between Celsius, Kelvin, and Fahrenheit.
  • Define specific heat capacity and explain its importance.
  • Apply the heat energy equation to solve quantitative problems.

2.1 Understanding Temperature

Temperature is a physical property that describes the thermal condition of a substance. For an ideal gas, temperature is directly related to the average translational kinetic energy of its particles.

When the temperature of a substance increases, the average kinetic energy of its particles generally increases. When its temperature decreases, the average kinetic energy generally decreases.

Important Concept

Temperature indicates how hot or cold a substance is. It is not the same as the total thermal energy contained in an object.

2.2 Understanding Heat

Heat is energy transferred between two systems or objects because of a temperature difference. Under ordinary conditions, energy transfers spontaneously from a higher-temperature object to a lower-temperature object.

Heat transfer continues until the objects reach thermal equilibrium, assuming no other energy interactions interfere.

Temperature

A property describing the thermal state of a substance.

SI unit: Kelvin (K)

Heat

Energy transferred because of a temperature difference.

SI unit: Joule (J)

2.3 Temperature Scales

Temperature can be expressed using different measurement scales. The three commonly used scales are Celsius, Kelvin, and Fahrenheit.

Celsius to Kelvin

K = °C + 273.15

Kelvin to Celsius

°C = K − 273.15

Celsius to Fahrenheit

°F = (°C × 9/5) + 32

Fahrenheit to Celsius

°C = (°F − 32) × 5/9

2.4 Specific Heat Capacity

Specific heat capacity is the amount of energy required to raise the temperature of one kilogram of a substance by one kelvin, without a change in phase.

Different substances have different specific heat capacities. For example, water has a relatively high specific heat capacity, which means it requires considerable energy to change its temperature.

Specific Heat Capacity Equation

Q = mcΔT

Where:

  • Q = heat transferred (J)
  • m = mass of the substance (kg)
  • c = specific heat capacity (J/kg·K)
  • ΔT = final temperature − initial temperature (K or °C difference)

2.5 Worked Example

A 0.5 kg quantity of water is heated from 25°C to 45°C. If the specific heat capacity of water is 4,186 J/kg·K, calculate the heat absorbed.

Given:

m = 0.5 kg c = 4,186 J/kg·K ΔT = 45 − 25 = 20 K

Solution:

Q = mcΔT Q = (0.5)(4,186)(20) Q = 41,860 J

Therefore, the water absorbs 41,860 joules of heat energy.

2.6 Factors Affecting Heat Transfer

  • Mass: Greater mass requires more energy for the same temperature change, if the material is the same.
  • Specific heat capacity: Substances with higher specific heat capacities require more energy for the same mass and temperature change.
  • Temperature change: A greater temperature change requires more energy transfer under the assumptions of the equation.

2.7 Real-World Applications

  • Water is used in some cooling systems because of its high specific heat capacity.
  • Cooking requires thermal energy to increase the temperature of food.
  • Weather and climate are influenced by the different heating and cooling rates of land and water.
  • Thermometers measure temperature changes in scientific and everyday applications.
  • Industrial heating systems use heat calculations to manage energy requirements.

Module Summary

Temperature describes the thermal state of a substance, while heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. Specific heat capacity describes how much energy is required to change a substance's temperature. The equation Q = mcΔT helps calculate heat transfer when there is no phase change and the specific heat capacity is treated as constant.

MODULE 03 | THERMAL ENERGY TRANSFER

Heat Transfer

Explore how thermal energy moves through solids, liquids, gases, and empty space through conduction, convection, and radiation.

College Level Estimated Time: 40–50 Minutes

Learning Objectives

At the end of this module, students should be able to:

  • Define heat transfer and explain its direction.
  • Differentiate conduction, convection, and radiation.
  • Explain the mechanisms involved in each method.
  • Apply basic heat transfer equations to scientific problems.
  • Identify heat transfer mechanisms in everyday situations.

3.1 Introduction to Heat Transfer

Heat transfer is the movement of thermal energy from one object, substance, or region to another because of a temperature difference.

In spontaneous heat transfer, energy moves from a higher-temperature region toward a lower-temperature region until thermal equilibrium is reached, provided no external energy input changes the process.

Fundamental Principle

Heat transfer occurs through three primary mechanisms: conduction, convection, and radiation. More than one mechanism may occur at the same time.

3.2 Conduction

Conduction is the transfer of thermal energy through direct interactions between neighboring particles or through mobile electrons in certain materials. The material does not undergo bulk movement as a whole during conduction.

Conduction is especially important in solids. Metals are generally good thermal conductors because they contain mobile electrons that help transfer energy.

Mechanism

Energy is transferred through particle interactions and, in metals, mobile electrons.

Common Materials

Copper, aluminum, iron, steel, wood, plastic, and other solids.

Fourier's Law of Heat Conduction

Heat Transfer Rate

Q/t = kAΔT/L

Where:

  • Q/t = rate of heat transfer (W)
  • k = thermal conductivity (W/m·K)
  • A = cross-sectional area (m²)
  • ΔT = temperature difference (K)
  • L = material thickness or length (m)

This equation gives the magnitude of the heat transfer rate for steady, one-dimensional conduction through a uniform plane wall.

3.3 Convection

Convection is the transfer of thermal energy through the movement of fluids, including liquids and gases. It involves both energy transfer between particles and the bulk movement of the fluid.

When a fluid is heated, its density may decrease and it may rise. Cooler, denser fluid may then move downward, producing circulation called natural convection.

Natural Convection

Fluid motion develops because of density differences caused by temperature variations.

Example: Circulation of water in a heated pot.

Forced Convection

Fluid motion is produced by an external device such as a fan or pump.

Example: A fan cooling a warm computer.

Newton's Law of Cooling

Convective Heat Transfer Rate

Q/t = hA(Ts − T∞)

Where:

  • h = convective heat transfer coefficient (W/m²·K)
  • A = exposed surface area (m²)
  • Ts = surface temperature (K)
  • T∞ = surrounding fluid temperature (K)

3.4 Radiation

Radiation is the transfer of thermal energy through electromagnetic waves. Unlike conduction and convection, radiation does not require a material medium and can occur through a vacuum.

All objects with a temperature above absolute zero emit thermal radiation. The amount and distribution of emitted radiation depend on the object's temperature and surface properties.

Mechanism

Energy travels through electromagnetic waves, including infrared radiation.

Examples

Sunlight reaching Earth and warmth felt near a hot surface.

Stefan–Boltzmann Law

Radiative Heat Transfer Rate

P = εσA(T⁴ − Tsurr⁴)

Where:

  • P = net radiative heat transfer rate (W)
  • ε = surface emissivity
  • σ = Stefan–Boltzmann constant (5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W/m²·K⁴)
  • A = radiating surface area (m²)
  • T = absolute surface temperature (K)
  • Tsurr = absolute surrounding temperature (K)

This expression assumes a surface exchanging radiation with a large surrounding environment.

3.5 Comparing the Three Methods

Conduction

Direct energy transfer through particle interactions.

Requires material contact.

Convection

Energy transfer involving bulk fluid movement.

Occurs in liquids and gases.

Radiation

Energy transfer through electromagnetic waves.

Can occur through a vacuum.

3.6 Worked Example: Conduction

A metal plate has a thermal conductivity of 200 W/m·K, a surface area of 0.02 m², and a thickness of 0.05 m. Its two surfaces have temperatures of 100°C and 40°C. Calculate the steady heat transfer rate.

Given:

k = 200 W/m·K A = 0.02 m² ΔT = 100 − 40 = 60 K L = 0.05 m

Solution:

Q/t = kAΔT/L Q/t = (200)(0.02)(60)/0.05 Q/t = 4,800 W

The steady heat transfer rate through the plate is 4,800 watts.

3.7 Real-World Applications

  • Insulation reduces unwanted heat transfer in buildings.
  • Metal cookware uses conduction to transfer energy to food.
  • Fans and ventilation systems enhance forced convection.
  • Solar thermal collectors absorb radiation from sunlight.
  • Radiators and heat exchangers manage thermal energy in machines.

Module Summary

Heat transfer occurs through conduction, convection, and radiation. Conduction involves particle interactions, convection involves fluid motion, and radiation involves electromagnetic waves. These mechanisms are essential in engineering, environmental science, and everyday technology.

MODULE 04 | ENERGY CONSERVATION

Internal Energy and Work

Explore how energy is stored within thermodynamic systems and how heat and work cause changes in internal energy.

College Level Estimated Time: 40–50 Minutes

Learning Objectives

At the end of this module, students should be able to:

  • Define internal energy and identify its microscopic contributions.
  • Explain heat and work as modes of energy transfer.
  • State and interpret the First Law of Thermodynamics.
  • Distinguish work done by a system from work done on a system.
  • Solve numerical problems involving changes in internal energy.

4.1 Understanding Internal Energy

Internal energy is the total microscopic energy associated with the particles that make up a thermodynamic system. It includes microscopic kinetic energy, such as molecular translation, rotation, and vibration, as well as microscopic potential energy arising from interactions between particles.

Internal energy is a state property. Its change depends on the initial and final states of the system, rather than the particular path taken between those states.

Important Concept

Internal energy is different from the total macroscopic kinetic and potential energy of a system moving through its surroundings.

4.2 Factors Affecting Internal Energy

Temperature

Changes in temperature often change the average microscopic kinetic energy of particles.

Phase and Molecular Arrangement

Changes in phase and particle interactions can change the microscopic potential energy.

Amount of Substance

The total internal energy generally depends on the amount of material in the system.

State of the System

Pressure, volume, temperature, and composition can help determine the system's internal energy.

4.3 Understanding Work in Thermodynamics

Work is energy transferred between a system and its surroundings through an organized interaction. In a gas contained in a movable piston, expansion or compression can involve pressure-volume work.

When a gas expands against an external pressure, it can perform work on its surroundings. When the gas is compressed, work is done on the system.

Pressure-Volume Work

W = ∫ Pext dV

For constant external pressure:

W = Pext(V₂ − V₁)

W = work done by the system, Pext = external pressure, and V₂ − V₁ = change in volume.

4.4 The First Law of Thermodynamics

The First Law of Thermodynamics is a statement of the conservation of energy. It explains that the change in a system's internal energy is equal to the heat added to the system minus the work done by the system.

First Law of Thermodynamics

ΔU = Q − W
  • ΔU = change in internal energy (J)
  • Q = heat added to the system (J)
  • W = work done by the system (J)

Sign convention: heat entering the system is positive, while work performed by the system is positive.

4.5 Understanding the Sign Convention

Positive Heat (Q)

Heat enters the system, increasing the energy transferred into it.

Negative Heat (Q)

Heat leaves the system and transfers to the surroundings.

Positive Work (W)

The system performs work on its surroundings.

Negative Work (W)

Work is performed on the system by its surroundings.

4.6 Special Thermodynamic Processes

Isothermal Process

A process occurring at constant temperature. For an ideal gas, internal energy does not change during an isothermal process.

Adiabatic Process

A process in which no heat is transferred between the system and surroundings.

Q = 0

Isochoric Process

A process occurring at constant volume. Boundary pressure-volume work is zero.

W = 0

Isobaric Process

A process occurring at constant pressure. Boundary work may occur when volume changes.

4.7 Worked Example

A thermodynamic system receives 800 J of heat and performs 300 J of work on its surroundings. Calculate the change in its internal energy.

Given:

Q = +800 J W = +300 J

Solution:

ΔU = Q − W ΔU = 800 − 300 ΔU = +500 J

The internal energy increases by 500 joules.

4.8 Another Worked Example

A gas receives 250 J of heat while 100 J of work is performed on the gas. Determine its change in internal energy.

Given:

Q = +250 J W = −100 J

Solution:

ΔU = Q − W ΔU = 250 − (−100) ΔU = +350 J

The internal energy increases by 350 joules.

4.9 Real-World Applications

  • Engines convert energy into mechanical work.
  • Compressors require work input to increase gas pressure.
  • Industrial boilers transfer heat into working fluids.
  • Refrigeration systems use work to move heat between regions.
  • Power plants use energy transformations to generate electricity.

Module Summary

Internal energy represents microscopic energy stored within a system. Heat and work are two ways energy can cross the system boundary. The First Law of Thermodynamics expresses energy conservation through ΔU = Q − W using the convention that work done by the system is positive.

MODULE 05 | ENERGY QUALITY

Entropy and Energy Efficiency

Discover how energy becomes dispersed, why natural processes have a preferred direction, and how thermodynamic systems use energy efficiently.

College Level Estimated Time: 40–50 Minutes

Learning Objectives

At the end of this module, students should be able to:

  • Define entropy and explain its importance in thermodynamics.
  • Describe the Second Law of Thermodynamics.
  • Calculate entropy changes in simple reversible processes.
  • Explain thermal efficiency and energy losses.
  • Calculate the efficiency of heat engines and Carnot engines.

5.1 Understanding Entropy

Entropy is a thermodynamic state property that describes the dispersal of energy and the number of microscopic arrangements consistent with a system's macroscopic state.

In statistical mechanics, entropy is related to the number of possible microscopic arrangements of a system.

Boltzmann's Entropy Equation

S = kB ln Ω
  • S = entropy (J/K)
  • kB = Boltzmann constant
  • Ω = number of accessible microstates

5.2 The Second Law of Thermodynamics

The Second Law explains the direction of natural processes. For an isolated system, total entropy cannot decrease. It remains constant in an ideal reversible process and increases in an irreversible process.

Important Principle

The total entropy of an isolated system increases or remains constant.

5.3 Entropy Change

For a reversible heat transfer occurring at a constant temperature, entropy change can be calculated using the following equation:

Entropy Change

ΔS = Qrev / T
  • ΔS = entropy change (J/K)
  • Qrev = reversible heat transfer (J)
  • T = absolute temperature (K)

Temperature must be expressed in kelvin.

5.4 Entropy in Different Processes

Reversible Process

An ideal process that can be reversed without leaving a net change in the system and surroundings.

Irreversible Process

A real process involving effects such as friction, uncontrolled expansion, or heat transfer across a finite temperature difference.

Isolated System

A system that exchanges neither matter nor energy with its surroundings.

Entropy Generation

Entropy produced by irreversibilities within a process. It is never negative.

5.5 Heat Engines and Thermal Efficiency

A heat engine operates by receiving energy from a high-temperature source, converting part of that energy into useful work, and rejecting the remaining heat to a lower-temperature sink.

Heat Engine Efficiency

η = (Wout / Qin) × 100%

Since Wout = Qin − Qout:

η = [(Qin − Qout) / Qin] × 100%
  • η = thermal efficiency
  • Wout = useful work output
  • Qin = heat supplied
  • Qout = heat rejected

5.6 Carnot Efficiency

The Carnot cycle represents an ideal reversible heat engine operating between two thermal reservoirs. It establishes the maximum possible efficiency for an engine operating between those temperatures.

Carnot Efficiency

η = (1 − Tc / Th) × 100%
  • Tc = cold reservoir temperature (K)
  • Th = hot reservoir temperature (K)

The Carnot efficiency is a theoretical maximum, not the efficiency of every real-world engine.

5.7 Worked Example: Entropy Change

A system reversibly absorbs 600 J of heat at a constant temperature of 300 K. Calculate its entropy change.

Given:

Qrev = 600 J T = 300 K

Solution:

ΔS = Qrev / T ΔS = 600 / 300 ΔS = 2 J/K

5.8 Worked Example: Thermal Efficiency

A heat engine receives 1,500 J of heat and produces 450 J of useful work. Determine its thermal efficiency.

Given:

Qin = 1,500 J Wout = 450 J

Solution:

η = (Wout / Qin) × 100% η = (450 / 1500) × 100% η = 30%

5.9 Real-World Applications

  • Improving the efficiency of power plants.
  • Reducing energy losses in engines.
  • Designing efficient refrigeration systems.
  • Understanding energy conservation in industrial processes.
  • Developing more efficient heating and cooling systems.

Module Summary

Entropy helps describe the direction of thermodynamic processes. The Second Law states that the total entropy of an isolated system cannot decrease. Thermal efficiency measures how effectively supplied energy is converted into useful work. The Carnot cycle establishes the theoretical maximum efficiency between two temperatures.

Applications of Thermodynamics

Connect the principles of thermodynamics to machines, transportation, electricity generation, refrigeration, and everyday technology.

College Level Estimated Time: 40–50 Minutes

Learning Objectives

At the end of this module, students should be able to:

  • Identify common applications of thermodynamics.
  • Explain the operation of basic heat engines.
  • Describe refrigeration and heat pump systems.
  • Explain thermodynamics in power generation.
  • Apply thermodynamic principles to everyday situations.

6.1 Thermodynamics in Everyday Life

Thermodynamics plays an important role in modern technology. It explains how energy is transferred and transformed in machines, appliances, transportation systems, and industrial processes.

Understanding these principles helps engineers develop systems that are more efficient, reliable, and sustainable.

6.2 Internal Combustion Engines

Internal combustion engines convert the chemical energy of fuel into thermal energy and then mechanical work. They are commonly used in automobiles, motorcycles, generators, and other machines.

Intake

Air or an air-fuel mixture enters the engine cylinder.

Compression

The piston compresses the contents of the cylinder.

Power

Combustion increases gas pressure, pushing the piston and producing work.

Exhaust

Combustion products leave the cylinder.

6.3 Refrigeration Systems

Refrigerators transfer heat from a low-temperature region to a higher-temperature surrounding environment by using external work.

A common refrigeration system uses a refrigerant that circulates through four major components.

Evaporator

The refrigerant absorbs heat from the refrigerated space.

Compressor

Work is supplied to increase the refrigerant's pressure.

Condenser

The refrigerant releases heat to the surroundings.

Expansion Valve

The refrigerant pressure is reduced before it returns to the evaporator.

Coefficient of Performance of a Refrigerator

COP = Qc / Win
  • COP = coefficient of performance
  • Qc = heat removed from the cold space
  • Win = work input

6.4 Heat Pumps

A heat pump transfers thermal energy from a lower-temperature source to a higher-temperature space. It uses external work to accomplish this transfer.

Heat Pump Coefficient of Performance

COPHP = Qh / Win
  • Qh = heat delivered to the warm space
  • Win = work input

6.5 Power Plants and Electricity Generation

Many power plants use thermodynamic cycles to convert thermal energy into mechanical work and eventually electrical energy.

In a steam power plant, heat is used to produce high-pressure steam. The steam expands through a turbine, which drives an electrical generator.

  • Boiler: transfers heat to water and produces steam.
  • Turbine: converts steam energy into mechanical work.
  • Generator: converts mechanical energy into electrical energy.
  • Condenser: removes heat and condenses steam into water.
  • Pump: moves water back toward the boiler.

6.6 Solar Thermal Energy

Solar thermal systems collect radiation from the Sun and convert it into useful thermal energy. This energy may be used for water heating, industrial heating, or electricity generation.

Science in the Community

Solar water heaters are examples of how heat transfer and energy conversion can be applied to reduce dependence on conventional energy sources.

6.7 Thermodynamics in the Human Body

The human body also follows thermodynamic principles. Metabolic processes transform chemical energy from food into useful work and thermal energy.

  • Metabolism involves chemical energy transformations.
  • Blood circulation helps distribute thermal energy.
  • Sweating supports heat loss through evaporation.
  • Heat exchange with the environment affects body temperature.

6.8 Worked Example: Refrigerator Performance

A refrigerator removes 600 J of heat from its interior while consuming 200 J of work. Calculate its coefficient of performance.

Given:

Qc = 600 J Win = 200 J

Solution:

COP = Qc / Win COP = 600 / 200 COP = 3

6.9 Worked Example: Heat Pump

A heat pump delivers 2,400 J of heat to a room while consuming 600 J of work. Determine its coefficient of performance.

Given:

Qh = 2,400 J Win = 600 J

Solution:

COPHP = Qh / Win COPHP = 2,400 / 600 COPHP = 4

6.10 Real-World Applications and Innovations

Electricity Generation

Thermodynamic cycles help power thermal electricity plants.

Transportation

Engines transform energy into mechanical motion.

Food Preservation

Refrigeration slows food deterioration by maintaining lower temperatures.

Renewable Energy

Solar thermal technologies collect and use energy from sunlight.

Module Summary

Thermodynamics has important applications in engines, refrigerators, heat pumps, power plants, solar thermal systems, and the human body. These technologies demonstrate how heat, work, and internal energy interact in practical systems.
